PART 1: WHAT IS THE PROBLEM? (Explain this first to professor)
================================================================

Imagine an employee at DRDO takes a photo of a classified missile
blueprint. He cannot email the PDF directly — security will catch it.

So instead, he opens a normal-looking JPG photo of a cricket match.
He uses free software to HIDE the blueprint data inside that photo's
pixels. The photo still looks 100% normal to human eyes. He emails
it out. Antivirus does not catch it. Firewall does not catch it.
He walks out with classified data.

This technique is called STEGANOGRAPHY — hiding secret data inside
innocent image files.

YOUR PROJECT builds the detector that catches this.

Real-world example of how hiding works:
  - Normal pixel value  = 200  (colour value, 0-255 range)
  - Attacker changes it = 201  (last bit changed = 1 bit of data)
  - Human eye sees ZERO difference
  - But across millions of pixels = you can hide a whole document
  - This specific method is called LSB (Least Significant Bit) hiding

PART 3: YOUR SOLUTION — THREE-INSPECTOR SYSTEM
================================================================

Think of your model like a THREE-INSPECTOR TEAM checking the
same image simultaneously:

INSPECTOR A — Pixel Branch (Branch A)
  - Looks directly at raw pixel values
  - Uses SRM filters to amplify tiny bit-level changes
  - Catches: LSB steganography
  - Analogy: Like a pixel-level microscope
  - Implemented as: CNN on pixel residual maps

INSPECTOR B — Frequency Branch (Branch B)
  - Converts the image into frequency domain (DCT)
  - Same math that JPEG compression uses
  - Catches: JPEG-domain / DCT steganography
  - Analogy: Like an X-ray scanner for image frequencies
  - Implemented as: CNN on DCT coefficient maps

INSPECTOR C — Statistics Branch (Branch C)
  - Checks statistical patterns of the image
  - Does the histogram look natural? Is chi-square test normal?
  - Catches: classical statistical-anomaly stego (see honesty
    note in Part 17 about GAN-based stego claims)
  - Analogy: Like a financial auditor checking for anomalies
  - Implemented as: Simple MLP on handcrafted stat features

SENIOR INSPECTOR — Fusion Layer
  - Gets all three branch outputs (feature vectors)
  - Combines them using Dense layers + Softmax
  - Gives final verdict: CLEAN or STEGO
  - Also gives a confidence percentage (e.g. 94.7% stego)

No existing single tool does all three together in this specific
combination. That combination is your innovation.

PART 15: NEW — IS THIS PATENTABLE IN INDIA?
================================================================

SHORT ANSWER: Partially, yes — IF drafted correctly. A bare
software algorithm alone is hard to patent in India. Software tied
to a concrete hardware deployment, producing a measurable technical
effect, has a real and reasonable chance.

15.1 — The legal starting point: Section 3(k)
The Indian Patents Act, 1970, Section 3(k), excludes "a mathematical
or business method or a computer programme per se or algorithms"
from being treated as patentable inventions. This was added in 2002
to satisfy the WTO TRIPS agreement and refined in 2005.

The critical phrase is "per se." This means only a STANDALONE
algorithm — software doing nothing but abstract computation — is
excluded. Software that produces a genuine technical effect, or
that works together with hardware to solve a technical problem, is
not automatically barred.

15.2 — What actually counts as a "technical effect"?
In Ferid Allani vs Union of India (Delhi High Court, 2019), the
court listed recognised examples of technical effect: higher
processing speed, reduced hard-disk access time, more economical
use of memory, a more efficient database search strategy, and an
improved user interface. Notice how closely your CPU-only,
quantized, faster-inference deployment claim (Part 6) matches this
exact list — smaller model, faster inference, and running without
a GPU are concrete, measurable technical effects, not just an
abstract classification idea.

15.3 — Does adding a hardware component genuinely raise your
chances? Largely yes, with one honest caveat.
In practice, patent examiners find it far easier to grant an
application when the claim is written around a concrete apparatus
or system — for example, "a steganalysis apparatus comprising a
quantized multi-branch classifier configured to execute on a
CPU-only, air-gapped computing unit" — rather than around an
abstract "method of classifying an image." Case law such as
Lava v Ericsson has stressed that an invention should enhance
hardware functionality to be more comfortably patentable.

The honest caveat: courts have also clarified (Ferid Allani and the
Microsoft Technology Licensing case) that a "novel hardware"
component is not a strict, mandatory legal requirement — a pure
software invention with a clearly demonstrated technical effect can,
in principle, be patented without introducing any new hardware.
So hardware is not legally compulsory, but tying your claims to
specific hardware constraints gives a patent agent a much easier,
more concrete argument to put in front of an examiner, and
meaningfully lowers your real-world rejection risk. This matches
your instinct that hardware involvement helps — it does, practically,
even though it is not the only legal path available.

15.4 — What part of YOUR project is the strongest patent candidate?
Be careful here: it is NOT the general "three-branch fusion" idea by
itself. Multiple recent papers already fuse pixel, frequency, and
statistical or noise-based features for detection tasks (see Part 17)
— a patent examiner's prior-art search will find these.

The stronger, more defensible claim is the SPECIFIC COMBINATION of:
  (a) the particular pixel + DCT + statistical fusion architecture
  (b) PLUS the CPU-only INT8-quantized deployment pipeline
  (c) PLUS the defense-context risk-scoring module (privilege,
      time-of-transfer, destination IP)
  all functioning together as one integrated system aimed at
  air-gapped defense environments.
That integrated combination — not any single piece of it — is what
a patent agent should draft narrow, specific claims around.

15.5 — CRI Guidelines 2025
The Indian Patent Office's Guidelines for Examination of Computer
Related Inventions were updated on 29 July 2025, giving a stepwise
assessment framework: examiners first identify the actual substance
of the invention, then check whether it is a bare algorithm or has a
genuine technical implementation, before making a patentability
decision. Knowing this sequence in advance helps you (or your
patent agent) write a specification that speaks directly to each
step, rather than leaving the examiner to infer the technical effect.

PART 16: NEW — FUTURE PROBLEMS YOU WILL FACE
================================================================

16.1 — THE BIGGEST ONE: publishing your paper vs filing a patent
India follows an "absolute novelty" standard. This means ANY public
disclosure, anywhere in the world, before your patent filing date
can legally destroy your own chances of patenting it — even when
you are the one who disclosed it.

This includes:
  - Uploading your project report or paper publicly
  - Presenting it at a conference, symposium, or open seminar
  - A public GitHub repository containing your model or method
  - Your final-year viva presentation, if open to the public or press
  - Even a detailed LinkedIn post or blog describing your method

There is NO general 12-month grace period in India (unlike the US).
Sections 29-34 of the Patents Act give only narrow, specific
exceptions — for example Section 31, covering government-notified
exhibitions or a paper read before a recognised "learned society" —
and even then you must still file within 12 months of that
disclosure. Most college journals, most student conferences, and
most viva presentations do not reliably qualify for this narrow
exception.

PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY: Part 14 lists "Project report / paper" as a
deliverable. If you want to keep the option of patenting open, you
must file a patent application (a provisional application is
enough to start) BEFORE that report or paper is published or
presented anywhere in public. File first, publish second. This is
the single most important timing decision in your whole project,
and it is very easy to get backwards by accident.

16.2 — Section 35: secrecy directions (your DRDO framing is relevant)
Your own professor pitch (Part 12) explicitly targets DRDO and
air-gapped defense infrastructure. Chapter VII (Sections 35-42) of
the Patents Act allows the Controller General of Patents, Designs
and Trade Marks to flag any application considered "relevant for
defence purposes" and issue a secrecy direction. If that happens:
  - the application cannot be published,
  - cannot be granted,
  - cannot be filed abroad,
  - and stays in that suspended state until the government lifts
    the direction, with no fixed deadline for that review.
This is a national-security screening step, not a punishment or a
rejection — but it is a real, foreseeable possibility precisely
because you are framing the invention around defense use, and it
is worth knowing about going in rather than discovering it later.

16.3 — Prior art already exists for "fusing multiple domains"
Be honest with yourselves here: the general idea of fusing pixel,
frequency, and statistical (or noise-based) features for detection
is not new by itself. Several 2024-2025 papers already explore
variations of this (see Part 17 for specifics). If your patent
claims are written broadly as "a system that fuses pixel, frequency,
and statistical features," an examiner can and likely will reject
them by citing this existing published research. Any patent you
pursue needs narrow, specific claims around your particular
combination (Part 15.4), not the general fusion concept.

16.4 — Cost, time, and whether a full patent is worth it here
A realistic Indian patent timeline: a provisional filing is quick
and relatively inexpensive, but a full grant typically takes
several years due to examination backlog, and then requires renewal
fees for up to 20 years to keep the patent alive. Filing costs
(agent fees plus government fees) commonly run from the tens of
thousands into lakhs of rupees depending on complexity, before any
opposition proceedings are even considered. For a 2nd-year college
project, it is worth asking plainly: does a full patent serve your
actual goals, or would a cheap provisional filing (which secures
your priority date for 12 months) combined with your published
paper achieve what you actually want?

16.5 — Who owns the patent? Check your college's IP policy first
Many Indian institutions have an internal IP policy that assigns
ownership of student project inventions to the institution itself,
particularly where college infrastructure, faculty supervision, or
institution-provided compute resources were used. Before assuming
that you and your teammate personally hold the patent rights, check
your college's student IP or technology-transfer policy. This step
is very commonly overlooked until it becomes a dispute.

16.6 — Geography: an Indian patent only protects you in India
If you want protection in the US, EU, or elsewhere, you need
separate national filings, or a single PCT international
application filed within 12 months of your first filing — which is
significantly more expensive. This is generally only worth pursuing
if there is real commercial or defense-contractor interest.

16.7 — A patent does not stop a determined competitor
Even a granted patent only blocks the specific claimed
implementation. A competitor can often "design around" it — for
example, by swapping your fusion method for a slightly different
combination technique, or using a different quantization scheme —
and legally avoid infringing. A patent protects your specific
claimed technical implementation, not a monopoly over the general
idea of "detect steganography using multiple branches."

PART 17: NEW — UPDATED RESEARCH GAP (papers found, July 2026)
================================================================

Your original research gap (Xu-Net = pixel-only, Yedroudj-Net =
fails after resize/compression, antivirus = completely blind) is
still accurate and worth keeping in your report. Here is what more
recent literature adds, to make this section stronger and properly
cited:

- A large 2024/2025 ACM Computing Surveys review, covering several
  hundred references on steganography and steganalysis, concluded
  that the biggest unresolved problem in the field is
  generalisability: most detectors are built for one specific
  hiding method and perform poorly against others. This is exactly
  the gap your three-branch design is aimed at, and it is a strong,
  citable source to justify why your problem matters in your
  report's related-work section.

- A late-2025 ScienceDirect review of image and video steganalysis
  techniques found that even modern deep-learning detectors still
  struggle with dataset imbalance and weak generalisation across
  different embedding methods, confirming this remains genuinely
  open rather than already solved.

- A 2025 Frontiers in Artificial Intelligence study specifically
  tested how steganalysis models handle real-world image
  transformations such as compression, resizing, and added noise,
  and found current models still degrade noticeably under these
  conditions. This directly backs your document's original
  criticism of Yedroudj-Net, giving you a recent, citable source
  for that exact claim rather than relying on the original paper
  alone.

- Honesty check for Innovation 3 (CPU-only deployment): a
  November 2025 paper specifically studied steganalysis under
  limited computational resources, combining SRM filters with
  self-supervised learning to build a lightweight model. This means
  the "run steganalysis without a GPU" angle is a genuinely active
  research area already — you did not invent an empty niche. Very
  few papers, however, combine this with a three-branch fusion
  detector AND a defense-context risk-scoring layer at the same
  time. That specific combined package is your real, defensible
  research gap, not the CPU-deployment idea by itself.

- Several 2024-2025 papers in adjacent areas — audio steganalysis
  using multi-scale fusion with attention, multi-resolution feature
  fusion for image steganalysis, and RGB-plus-noise-view fusion for
  image manipulation detection using SRM and DCT features together
  — confirm that "fusing multiple feature branches" is an active,
  trending direction across steganalysis and closely related fields.
  This makes your core architectural idea credible and current, but
  it also means you should frame your novelty as the specific
  COMBINATION described below, not the general fusion concept on
  its own.

REFINED ONE-LINE RESEARCH GAP FOR YOUR REPORT:
"While recent work explores multi-branch feature fusion, lightweight
edge-deployable models, and robustness to image transformations as
separate research threads, no existing published system combines
all three — pixel, frequency, and statistical fusion; CPU-only
quantized deployment; and actionable defense-context risk scoring —
into one integrated, air-gapped-deployable steganalysis pipeline for
defense-sensitive environments."

A NOTE ON BRANCH C AND "GAN-BASED" STEGANOGRAPHY:
Classical statistical features (histogram, chi-square) were strong
tools against older, non-adaptive hiding methods, but they perform
weakly against modern adaptive steganography and are not a reliable
way to catch GAN-generated stego images. If a professor or examiner
pushes on this point, the defensible answer is that Branch C targets
classical statistical anomalies, while Branches A and B (backed by
learned CNN features) carry the load against more modern methods.
Avoid overselling Branch C as a GAN-detector in your report.

PART 18: NEW — STEP-BY-STEP IMPLEMENTATION PLAN (SIMPLE WORDS)
================================================================

This restates Parts 3, 4, and 9 as one plain, numbered checklist
you can literally follow, week by week.

STEP 1 — Learn the basics (Week 1)
  Both students learn Python, NumPy, PIL, and Git. No project code
  yet — just learning the tools you will need.

STEP 2 — Build the hiding tool yourselves (Week 2)
  Write a simple LSB steganography embedder, about 20 lines of
  Python. This is what lets you create your own labelled "stego"
  images later.

STEP 3 — Get and prepare the data (Week 3)
  Download the BOSS dataset (10,000 grayscale images). Use your
  Step 2 embedder to turn 5,000 of them into stego images. Keep the
  other 5,000 as clean. Double-check a sample of labels by hand
  before trusting the whole dataset.

STEP 4 — Look at the data with your own eyes (Week 4)
  Compute pixel residual maps — the tiny "difference" that hiding
  creates. Plot a handful. Confirm visually that hiding is really
  happening before you spend time training any model on it.

STEP 5 — Build ONE simple CNN first (Weeks 3-4, alongside Steps 3-4)
  Do not attempt all three branches at once. Build one plain 3-layer
  CNN. Get it training on the data from Step 3. Even 70% accuracy
  here is a fine milestone — it just proves your whole pipeline
  works end to end before you add complexity.

STEP 6 — Build Branch A: the pixel branch (Weeks 5-6)
  Add SRM filters before the CNN input. Tune the convolution layers.
  Aim for 85%+ accuracy on LSB detection alone.

STEP 7 — Build Branch B: the frequency branch (Weeks 5-6, parallel)
  Apply DCT on 8x8 image blocks using scipy. Build a separate CNN
  on these DCT coefficient maps. Aim for 80%+ accuracy on
  JPEG-domain stego.

STEP 8 — Build Branch C: the statistics branch (Week 7)
  Extract histogram and chi-square features by hand. Feed these
  into a small 3-layer MLP — this branch is deliberately simple.

STEP 9 — Test each branch alone first (Week 8)
  Before combining anything, write down each branch's solo accuracy
  number. You will need these for your comparison table later.

STEP 10 — Build the fusion layer (Weeks 9-10)
  Take the feature output from all three branches. Normalise each
  branch's output before combining them — skipping this step is the
  single most common reason fusion training fails to converge.
  Concatenate the three into one vector. Add Dense(128) then
  Dense(64) then Softmax. Fine-tune everything together. Aim for
  85-90% on the combined dataset — 85% is still a strong, honest
  result, so do not treat anything short of 90% as a failure.

STEP 11 — Evaluate properly (Week 11)
  Plot a confusion matrix. Calculate the ROC curve and AUC score.
  Compare your numbers against a simplified Xu-Net baseline that
  you build yourself. This comparison table becomes your main proof
  of contribution to show the professor.

STEP 12 — Shrink the model for CPU deployment (Week 11)
  Apply INT8 quantization with torch.quantization. Measure model
  size before and after (expect roughly 4x smaller) and inference
  time on an ordinary CPU laptop with no GPU. Expect a small,
  honest accuracy drop — budget for up to 2-5%, and report the real
  number rather than assuming it will be under 2%.

STEP 13 — Build the demo (Week 12)
  A small Streamlit app: upload an image, see CLEAN or STEGO with a
  confidence percentage. Keep it to 10-15 lines — it is a demo, not
  a shipped product.

STEP 14 — Write the report (start Week 8, finish Week 12)
  Do not leave this to the last week. Write the introduction and
  literature review during Month 1, while training runs are
  happening in the background. Add one new section per week from
  Week 8 onward.

STEP 15 — Decide your patent timing BEFORE Step 14 goes public
  If you want to keep the option of patenting your work open, file
  a provisional patent application (talk to your college's IP cell
  or a registered patent agent) BEFORE the report becomes public in
  any form — before uploading it, before any public presentation,
  before a public GitHub repository. This is the step most student
  teams forget, and it cannot be undone once the work is public.
